Part I: The Architecture of Kenyan Retail
Chapter 1: The Retail Continuum (The Physics of Physical Space)
To the absolute beginner, retail looks simple: buy goods at a low price, put them on a shelf, and sell them at a higher price. But a retail store is not a storage unit for boxes. It is a psychological funnel designed to manipulate human movement and capture cash flow.
In Kenya, retail formats do not evolve in a straight line. The massive Mega-Store does not replace the neighborhood corner shop. Instead, they exist at the exact same time, targeting the exact same consumer, just at different points in their monthly salary cycle. If you do not understand the specific "physics" of your store's size, you will bankrupt yourself trying to run a corner shop like a supermarket.
1.1 The Micro-Duka: The High-Velocity Survival Engine
A standard duka (kiosk) operates in a space often smaller than 100 square feet. To a beginner, it looks like a chaotic, informal setup. To an expert, it is a hyper-efficient cash engine built for the "Kadogo" (small) economy.
The Psychology: The primary duka customer is a daily wage earner, or a middle-class consumer needing an immediate emergency item at 8:00 PM. They cannot afford to lock up their money in a 30-day supply of groceries. They buy for the next 12 hours.
The Format Constraints: A duka uses a "Service Window" layout. The customer stands outside; the operator fetches the goods. This means the customer cannot browse.
The SKU Strategy (The Expert View): Because space is tiny, a duka can only stock about 150 items. You cannot stock slow-moving items here. A duka survives on Traffic Engines (like Brookside 500ml milk). In larger supermarkets, this milk is a "Loss Leader" sold at a negative margin (-7.88%) just to get people in the door. But in a duka, the operator applies a Convenience Premium—marking the price up slightly because the customer won't walk 3 kilometers in the dark to save 5 shillings.
The Trap: The operator uses that milk to bring the customer to the window. While the customer waits for change, they stare at the wire mesh. This is where the operator hangs the Impulse Closer (like a Mr. Berrys Lollipop at a 33% profit margin). The milk pays the distributor; the lollipop buys the operator's dinner.
1.2 The Estate Minimart: The Browsing Trap
The Minimart (500 to 2,000 square feet) sits inside residential estates. It is built for the "Mid-Week Top-Up." The customer comes in on a Wednesday evening for fresh bread, milk, and maybe a cleaning spray.
The Psychology: This is the first format where the customer holds a basket and walks inside the store. They transition from "immediate need" to "browsing."
The Format Constraints: This format gives the operator the "Illusion of Space." A beginner operator sees an empty shelf and thinks they need to fill it with variety.
The SKU Strategy (The Expert View): This is the most dangerous format in retail. It is the prime breeding ground for Cannibalization. Because the operator has space, a supplier will convince them to stock 15 different brands of mid-tier wine. But the customer walking in on a Wednesday just wants any decent red wine. By stocking 15 varieties, the operator doesn't sell more wine; they just spread the same number of sales across 15 bottles. The total revenue stays the same, but the operator has trapped 15 times more of their own cash in inventory that sits on the shelf gathering dust. Minimarts require ruthless "Assortment Capping"—if you only need 4 brands of wine to satisfy the customer, you strictly ban the other 11 from your store.
1.3 The Mega-Store: The Shadow Bank
The Mega-Store (10,000+ square feet) is a logistical beast. It does not survive on daily convenience; it survives on the Payday Pulse—the end-of-month salary cycle when consumers buy their monthly bulk items.
The Psychology: Destination shopping. The customer is willing to drive past ten dukas and three minimarts because the Mega-Store guarantees they can get everything on their list in one trip.
The Format Constraints: The biggest threat to a Mega-Store is the "Dead Zone." If a store is 20,000 square feet, a customer will naturally try to walk the shortest path, ignoring 80% of the aisles.
The SKU Strategy (The Expert View): To fix Dead Zones, experts use Anchor Dispersal. You take the heavy, negative-margin staples that everyone must buy—like 2kg Soko Flour and 5L Rina Cooking Oil—and you place them in the absolute furthest, opposite corners of the massive building. You force the customer to walk diagonally across the entire store, dragging them past the high-margin "Profit Stabilizers" (like premium spices and imported cosmetics).

Chapter 2: The Economics of the Shelf (Why Variety Kills)
The fastest way to spot a beginner in retail is to listen to how they talk about profit. A beginner talks about Gross Margin %. An expert talks about Velocity.
2.1 The Myth of the Flat Margin
If a supplier offers you a bottle of imported lotion with a 35% profit margin, and a loaf of everyday bread with a 10% profit margin, the beginner assumes the lotion is the better product. This is a fatal error. In retail, you do not pay your rent with percentages; you pay it with physical cash.
2.2 The GMROI Equation (The Ground Truth)
Experts measure success using GMROI (Gross Margin Return on Inventory Investment). This measures how many times a single product spins your cash in a 30-day cycle.
If you invest 10,000 KES into the 35% margin lotion, it sits on your shelf for 30 days. You make 3,500 KES, but your initial 10,000 KES was trapped for a full month.
If you invest 10,000 KES into the 10% margin bread, it sells out in one day. You make 1,000 KES. But because you have your 10,000 KES back, you buy more bread the next day. By the end of the month, that same 10,000 KES has spun 30 times, generating 30,000 KES in pure cash profit.
The fast, low-margin item is mathematically superior to the slow, high-margin item.
2.3 The Supplier-Funded Float
As a store grows from a Duka to a Mega-Store, it adds variety. Variety slows down Velocity (because specialty items don't sell every day). So how do Mega-Stores make money if their GMROI drops?
They stop using their own money. Because Mega-Stores move massive volumes of items like Coca-Cola and Brookside, they force suppliers to give them 30-day to 45-day credit terms.
The store receives the stock on Day 1, sells it all to the customer by Day 10, and holds the physical cash in their bank account for 20 days before they have to pay the supplier. They use this "free" supplier money to pay their staff, pay their rent, and fund their business expansion. A scaled retail store is actually a highly efficient, short-term shadow bank.

Chapter 3: The Deep Web of the Store
A beginner looks at a store as a collection of individual products. An expert looks at a store as a highly connected Neural Network. Products do not exist in isolation; they "link" to each other in the customer's shopping basket.
3.1 The Four Macro-Habitats
Your store is divided into four biological zones based on how products behave:
The Black Holes (e.g., Fresh Milk, Flour): These items make almost no profit, but they have the highest "Link Density"—meaning they drag customers into the store. You must place them at the very back of the store to force foot traffic.
The Margin Sanctuaries (e.g., Bread, Bottled Water): High profit, high loyalty. Customers rarely substitute these. You place them in the physical crossfire of the Black Holes so customers trip over them.
The Web Weavers (e.g., Sweets, Gum): Low revenue per item, but they connect to almost every basket demographic. You fracture this department and scatter it exclusively at the checkout counter where the customer's wallet is already open.
The Cannibal Zones (e.g., Wines, Spices): These zones contain hundreds of redundant items fighting for the same customer. You must strictly cap their physical shelf space.
3.2 Advanced Network Tactics (The Expert Playbook)
Once you understand the basic zones, you can weaponize the hidden links between products:
The Trojan Horse (Conditional Dependency): A supplier offers you a new, high-margin biscuit. But your checkout data shows this biscuit only sells when a customer is already buying Tier 1 Milk. The biscuit doesn't bring new customers; it acts as a parasite on the milk's foot traffic. The expert operator realizes this and refuses to buy the biscuit upfront, demanding the supplier place it on consignment instead.
The Bridge Node (Betweenness Centrality): How do you get a customer to walk from the Butchery (meat) to the Household Cleaning aisle (bleach)? They have no logical connection. The expert looks for the "Bridge Node"—in this case, Paper Serviettes. Customers buy serviettes for a weekend BBQ, and they also buy them during a monthly cleaning restock. By placing the Serviettes exactly halfway between the Butchery and the Bleach, you create a subconscious psychological trail that drags the customer into a new zone.
Asymmetric Cannibalization (Predator vs. Prey): What happens if you are short on cash and can only afford to stock one brand of Gin: the premium Gilbeys or the mid-tier Chrome? The beginner splits the money. The expert knows that if Chrome is out of stock, Chrome buyers will leave the store. But if Gilbeys is out of stock, Gilbeys buyers will sigh and "downgrade" to Chrome. Chrome is the Predator; Gilbeys is the Prey. The expert intentionally lets Gilbeys run out of stock, consolidating all the buyers into Chrome, saving 50% of their working capital without losing a single customer.
The Keystone Node: A 16 KES lollipop seems irrelevant compared to a 2,000 KES bale of flour. But the data shows that a parent will choose to shop at your Minimart over a competitor specifically because you have the exact sweet their child wants. If you run out of the lollipop, you don't just lose 16 KES; you lose the 2,000 KES flour basket attached to it. The expert protects Keystone nodes with the same urgency as high-ticket items.

Chapter 4: The GRN Checkpoint (Securing the Back Door)
To the absolute beginner, the Goods Received Note (GRN) process seems like basic administration. A delivery truck arrives, the driver hands you an invoice, your staff counts the boxes, signs the paper, and puts the goods on the shelf.
To the retail expert, the GRN Checkpoint is the most dangerous, highly contested square footage in the entire business.
The back door is where your working capital is physically stolen. It is where your gross margins are quietly diluted to zero. If your GRN process is flawed, your Point of Sale (POS) data becomes corrupted, and any automated replenishment system (like the OASIS engine) will trigger disastrous, inaccurate orders. You cannot algorithmicize a store that leaks at the foundation.
4.1 The Theater of Chaos (Weaponized Logistics)
The greatest vulnerability of a retail operator is the human psychology of their receiving staff. FMCG delivery drivers—especially those contracted by Tier 1 Apex Dictators—are under immense daily pressure. They have thirty stops to make, traffic is gridlocked, and they are impatient.
The Beginner View: The receiving clerk sees a stressed driver, a truck blocking the alleyway, and a massive stack of crates. Wanting to be efficient and accommodating, the clerk does a "spot check"—counting the top layer of boxes, assuming the layers underneath are full, and signing the invoice to get the truck out of the way.
The Expert View: The expert operator understands that suppliers weaponize this chaos. The rush is a deliberate tactic. If you allow the driver's urgency to dictate your receiving speed, you surrender your capital. The expert enforces a strict, unyielding physical protocol: the truck waits. No goods cross the threshold into the store until the driver's panic is entirely decoupled from the counting process.
4.2 The Ghost Delivery (The Mathematics of Shrinkage)
The most common method of capital extraction at the back door is the Ghost Delivery (also known as short-shipping).
Let us look at the mathematics of how a Ghost Delivery destroys your business, using a high-velocity staple from our network: BREAD (10% Margin).
The Setup: You order 100 loaves of bread. The supplier's invoice states 100 loaves.
The Execution: The delivery driver actually unloads 96 loaves. They hide the missing 4 loaves in the cab of the truck to sell to a roadside kiosk for pure profit later. Because the driver is rushing your clerk, your clerk signs the invoice acknowledging receipt of 100 loaves.
The Financial Reality: * You are now legally obligated to pay the supplier for 100 loaves.
To make your 10% profit margin, you needed to sell all 100 loaves.
Because you only received 96, the first 90 loaves you sell go entirely to paying the supplier's invoice.
By missing just 4 loaves on the receiving dock, the profit margin for the entire delivery of 100 loaves has been wiped out. Your staff will spend the whole day selling that bread, and the business will make exactly zero shillings.
4.3 Expiry Dumping (The Capital Trap)
While drivers from Tier 1 suppliers execute Ghost Deliveries, representatives from Tier 3 "Portfolio Trap" suppliers execute Expiry Dumping.
The Setup: A distributor has a warehouse full of premium cosmetics or imported spices that expire in 45 days. They cannot sell this to a Mega-Store because Mega-Stores have strict receiving algorithms that reject anything with less than 6 months of shelf life.
The Execution: The distributor targets the Estate Minimart. They bury the near-expiry stock at the bottom of the delivery carton, placing fresh stock on top.
The Financial Reality: The beginner clerk scans the top item, sees a 1-year expiry date, and accepts the box. The goods go onto the shelf. 45 days later, the operator must pull the expired goods off the shelf and throw them in the trash. The supplier has successfully transferred their dead warehouse stock onto your balance sheet, turning your retail store into a landfill for their mistakes.
4.4 The Blind Receiving Protocol (The Expert Execution)
How does a Master Operator stop Ghost Deliveries and Expiry Dumping? They remove human trust from the equation by implementing Blind Receiving.
The Beginner Flaw (Invoice-Led Receiving): The clerk holds the supplier's invoice that says "100 Cartons of Milk." The clerk looks at the pile, counts to 100, and checks a box. Human nature dictates that if you know the answer you are looking for, your brain will trick you into finding it, especially when rushed.
The Expert Fix (Blind Receiving): The clerk is never allowed to see the supplier's invoice. Instead, the OASIS system generates a blank "Blind GRN" on a handheld scanner.
The clerk must physically scan every barcode and manually type in the physical quantity they see before them (e.g., they count and enter "96").
They must manually type in the expiry date printed on the physical box.
The system then compares the clerk's blind count ("96") against the supplier's digital invoice ("100").
If there is a mismatch, the system locks the gate. The goods cannot be ingested into the store's inventory, and the driver is not allowed to leave until the discrepancy is physically resolved on the dock.
By forcing a Blind Receive, the operator breaks the psychology of the rushed driver and mathematically guarantees that the store only pays for the exact molecules of inventory that successfully crossed the threshold.

Chapter 5: High-Volume Logistics and Storage (The Science of the Backroom)
To the absolute beginner, the backroom is simply a closet—a place to hide excess boxes, damaged goods, and the chaotic mess of retail away from the customer's eyes. They view it as a necessary evil, a static holding pen for "stuff."
To the expert operator, the word "storage" is a financial curse.
Inventory sitting in the dark makes exactly zero shillings. A product only generates your Working Capital Cycle when it is placed under the bright lights of the shop floor, within arm’s reach of a buyer. The expert views the backroom not as a warehouse, but as a high-speed transit lounge. Every minute a product spends in the backroom is a minute it is not spinning cash.
5.1 The Anti-Storage Principle (Space vs. Velocity)
When negotiating for a new Minimart or Mega-Store location, the beginner often asks the landlord for a massive backroom, thinking more storage equals more security against stock-outs.
The Beginner View: A large backroom is safe. When the supplier offers a "Buy 10, Get 2 Free" promotion on a slow-moving Cannibal SKU (like a Tier 3 spice), the beginner accepts the deal and stuffs the extra boxes in the backroom for six months.
The Expert View: A massive backroom is a trap that encourages lazy buying. If you have the physical space to hide your mistakes, you will make them. The expert operators engineer their backrooms to be uncomfortably small—often no more than 15% to 20% of the total store footprint.
The Physics: By intentionally starving the backroom of space, you force your procurement system to rely on Velocity instead of hoarding. It forces you to negotiate "Just-in-Time" (JIT) deliveries from your Apex Suppliers. You do not want 14 days of BROOKSIDE MILK sitting in a massive cold room; you want 48 hours of milk spinning violently fast on the shop floor.
5.2 The Iron Law of FIFO (First-In, First-Out)
The single greatest destroyer of gross margin inside the backroom is poor stock rotation. It is a silent killer.
The Setup: You receive a delivery of BIO FRESH MILK today (expiring in 7 days). You already have 10 bottles in the fridge from yesterday (expiring in 6 days).
The Beginner Execution: The beginner staff member is rushed. They take the new crates, walk to the front of the fridge, and push the new, fresher milk directly in front of the older milk.
The Financial Reality: The customer will always buy the first bottle they touch. They buy the 7-day milk. The 6-day milk gets pushed further back into the dark. By the end of the week, those original 10 bottles expire. You have to pour them down the drain. You paid the supplier for milk that you threw away. Your staff's laziness just wiped out the profit margin for the entire dairy department for that day.
The Expert Execution (Physical Engineering): The expert does not rely on staff memory or willpower. They engineer the physical space to force FIFO.
Dairy fridges are built to be Rear-Loading. The staff member physically cannot put new milk in the front. They must walk into the cold room behind the shelf and load the new milk from the back, naturally pushing the older milk to the customer-facing front.
For dry goods like SOKO FLOUR, pallets are clearly marked with giant, neon "Receive Date" stickers. The backroom manager is penalized if a newer pallet is broken into before an older pallet is cleared.
5.3 Structural Mapping (The Velocity Grid)
A beginner organizes their backroom by supplier or by alphabetical order. They put all the Coca-Cola products together and all the Unilever products together.
The expert organizes the backroom using the exact same Tectonic Plate logic we used for the store floorplan. The backroom is mapped entirely by Velocity and Security.
The Fast Lane (Black Holes): High-velocity, bulky items ([[FLOUR]], [[SUGAR]], [[COOKING OIL]]) never go deep into the backroom. They are dropped immediately adjacent to the double doors leading to the shop floor. Because these items sell out multiple times a day, your staff cannot waste 5 minutes navigating a maze of boxes to restock them. Friction here kills your Traffic Engines.
The Vault (Margin Sanctuaries & Cannibals): High-margin, high-theft items ([[COSMETICS]], [[PREMIUM WINES]], [[SPIRITS]]) are physically segregated. They go into a locked chain-link cage inside the backroom. Only the store manager holds the key. If a bottle of Chrome Gin goes missing, it doesn't just hurt; it destroys the profit of 50 loaves of bread.
The Impulse Racks (Web Weavers): [[SWEETS]] and [[BATTERIES]] are tiny but hold immense value. Because they are scattered at the checkout chutes, the backroom holds them in highly organized, small-bin shelving.
5.4 The Quarantine Zone (Protecting the Algorithm)
The most critical, yet overlooked, physical space in the entire retail operation is the Quarantine Zone.
The Ground Truth: Things break. Sugar bales tear and leak. A customer drops a jar of coffee. A supplier delivers a dented can of tomatoes.
The Beginner Flaw: The beginner puts the broken jar back on the shelf, or shoves it into a random corner of the backroom. Because it is still physically in the building, the POS system thinks you have 1 unit of coffee left to sell. The OASIS Auto-Replenishment algorithm sees "1 unit available" and decides not to order more. The customer walks in, sees a broken jar, and leaves. You suffer a total stock-out because your system was lying to you.
The Expert Execution: The expert builds a taped-off, highly visible physical square in the backroom called the Quarantine Zone. The moment an item is damaged or flagged for supplier return, it is physically moved to this square. At 5:00 PM every single day, the manager scans every item in that square out of the system as "Damaged/Returned."
By physically and digitally purging the dead stock daily, the expert guarantees that the inventory numbers feeding the OASIS algorithm are 100% pure, true, and ready to trigger accurate reorders.

Chapter 6: Capital Flow and Supplier Dynamics (Weaponizing the Shelf)
To the absolute beginner, the supplier relationship is a hierarchy where the retailer is at the bottom. The distributor arrives, hands over a price list, dictates the credit terms, and tells the beginner what new products they must stock this month. The beginner accepts this because they believe they need the supplier’s goods to survive.
To the expert operator, the supplier is merely a guest renting highly valuable real estate. FMCG manufacturers have massive factories capable of producing millions of units, but those units are completely worthless until they cross the final three feet into a consumer's hand. You own those final three feet. You control the bottleneck.
6.1 The Asymmetry of Leverage (Redefining the Power Dynamic)
The first step in supplier negotiation is understanding your true position in the Tectonic Plates of the network.
The Apex Dictators (Tier 1): You cannot negotiate margin with Brookside or Coca-Cola. Their products are Black Holes; they dictate the traffic. If you threaten to delist them, your customers will simply walk to the Minimart next door. Your only negotiation lever here is Logistics and Float. You negotiate delivery frequency (forcing them to deliver daily to save your backroom space) and credit terms (fighting for 7 to 14 days to keep your capital from bleeding).
The Portfolio Traps (Tier 3): This is where you possess absolute power. A Tier 3 supplier selling a mid-tier spice or a new energy drink has zero consumer loyalty. If you remove their product, the customer will seamlessly substitute it for another brand. The expert operator uses the threat of substitution to extract maximum financial concessions.
6.2 Negotiating the Float (The Mathematics of Time)
We established in Chapter 2 that scaling a store requires a Supplier-Funded Float. But how do you actually negotiate it?
You do not ask a supplier for "better terms." You calculate your exact Net Capital Position (NCP) using the velocity data from your POS system.
$$NCP = \text{Supplier Credit Days} - \text{Days Inventory Outstanding (DIO)}$$
The Beginner Mistake: A supplier offers 14-day credit on a new brand of imported pasta. The beginner accepts. But the POS data shows that a batch of this pasta takes 45 days to sell out (DIO = 45).
$NCP = 14 - 45 = -31$
You are in a Negative Float. For 31 days, your own cash is trapped in that pasta. You are financing the supplier's business.
The Expert Execution: The expert runs the formula before signing the contract. They see the -31 day deficit. They sit across from the supplier and present the hard POS data: "Your product takes 45 days to spin. I require a positive float to cover my shelf space risk. I will only accept this product on 60-day credit terms, or pure consignment." If the supplier refuses, the expert denies them entry to the store.
6.3 Monetizing the Shelf: Slotting Fees and Backend Rebates
Beginners think the only way to make money is through the Gross Margin (buying at 80 KES, selling at 100 KES). Experts know that the Gross Margin is just the baseline. The real money is made in the hidden financial architecture: Rebates and Slotting Fees.
The Slotting Fee (Charging Rent): When a Tier 3 supplier brings a "Trojan Horse" product (a product entirely dependent on your store's existing foot traffic), the expert treats the shelf like an apartment. You charge a non-refundable "Slotting Fee" just to place the item on the shelf. This transfers the risk of the new product failing back to the manufacturer. If it doesn't sell, you still made money renting the space.
The Backend Volume Rebate: For Mid-Tier Stabilizers (like a premium bakery or a mid-level cosmetics brand), you negotiate a backend rebate. You agree to a standard wholesale price, but add a clause: "If I move 10,000 units of your product this quarter, you owe me a 5% cash rebate on the total volume." * This allows you to legally lower your retail price on the shop floor to crush your local competitors, while making your profit entirely on the backend check the supplier writes you at the end of the quarter.
6.4 Weaponized Delisting (The Vendor Purge)
The ultimate flex of operational leverage is the Vendor Purge.
The Setup: You have three different suppliers providing identical mid-tier 5-liter cooking oils. They are taking up massive physical space in your Cannibal Zone, tying up 300,000 KES in redundant working capital.
The Execution: You call all three suppliers into separate meetings. You show them the exact substitution data from your network. You explain that to the consumer, their oils are 100% identical.
The Ultimatum: "I am reducing my 5-liter oil category from three brands to one. I will award 100% of my store's volume to the supplier who gives me 30-day credit terms and a 3% backend volume rebate. The other two will be permanently delisted tomorrow."
Because you have the network data, you know you will not lose a single customer—they will simply buy the winning oil. You force the suppliers into a bidding war against each other. You consolidate your working capital, clean up your physical backroom, and secure highly lucrative financial terms, all without selling a single extra unit to the end consumer.

Chapter 7: Mapping the SKU Universe (Executing the Vendor Purge)
To the absolute beginner, a supplier catalog of 1,000 items looks like an opportunity. They believe that if they put all 1,000 items on the shelf, they will attract 1,000 different types of customers.
To the expert operator looking at the neural network data, a massive catalog is a declaration of war on their working capital. They do not generate foot traffic, they do not spin cash, and they only exist to cannibalize the sales of the 16% that actually work.

7.1 The Untouchables (Protect and Expand)
Before you cut, you must know what to protect. These are the Elite Stabilizers. They possess mathematically flawless catalogs.
The Network Data: Look at Kenya Breweries Ltd (KBL) and Aquamist Limited. The data shows KBL generated nearly 14 Million KES using only 36 SKUs. Aquamist generated 13.5 Million KES using only 40 SKUs.
The Neural Signature: High Revenue, Low SKU Count, Zero Substitution Edges. A customer who wants a Tusker does not substitute it for a soda. A customer who wants a 20L Aquamist refill does not buy 40 half-liter bottles instead.

7.2 The Necessary Evils (The Core vs. Tail Mandate)

8.2 The Mathematics of Affinity: Confidence and Lift

1. Confidence (The Predictability Score)
Confidence measures how often the Attachment is bought when the Anchor is in the basket.
$$Confidence(A \rightarrow B) = \frac{\text{Transactions with A and B}}{\text{Total Transactions with A}}$$
The Data: If Brookside Milk (A) is bought 1,000 times a week, and Kingsmil Bread (B) is in 400 of those exact baskets, the Confidence is 40%.
The Execution: A 40% Confidence score is a structural pillar. It means you can rely on the Bread to pay for the Milk's negative margin almost half the time.
2. Lift (The Halo Multiplier)
Lift measures how much the Anchor increases the normal sales of the Attachment.
$$Lift = \frac{Confidence(A \rightarrow B)}{\text{Expected Probability of B}}$$
The Data: If Kingsmil Bread normally ends up in 10% of all store baskets, but jumps to 40% when Brookside Milk is present, the Lift is 4.0.
The Execution: A Lift greater than 1.0 proves a true Halo Effect. A Lift of 4.0 means Brookside Milk makes Kingsmil Bread four times more likely to sell. This is why the expert operator physically bolts the bread rack to the side of the dairy fridge.

$$CR = \frac{\text{Volume Stolen from Existing SKUs}}{\text{Total Volume of New SKU}}$$

The Math: The volume stolen from Peptang is 20. The total volume of Zesty is 20.
$$CR = \frac{20}{20} = 1.0 \text{ (or 100\%)}$$
The Verdict: The Cannibalization Rate is 100%. The new product generated absolutely zero incremental lift for the store. It simply cannibalized the existing sales. The expert immediately delists Zesty, returns the stock, and frees up the working capital.
