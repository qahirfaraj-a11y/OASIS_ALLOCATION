# Chapter 9: Cannibalization and Redundancy (The Zero-Sum Game)

To the absolute beginner, an aisle packed with twenty different brands of tomato sauce, fifteen varieties of cooking oil, and ten flavors of potato crisps looks like excellent customer service. The beginner believes that by offering every possible option, they will capture every possible customer.
To the expert algorithmic operator, that same aisle looks like a financial massacre.
The expert knows that the physical stomach of the consumer is limited, and the wallet of the consumer is fixed. If a customer walks into your Minimart to buy a bottle of tomato sauce for their family's dinner, they are going to buy exactly one bottle. If you stock one brand, you sell one bottle. If you stock twenty brands, you still only sell one bottle—but you have trapped twenty times the working capital on your shelf to achieve the exact same financial result.
This is Cannibalization: when a product does not generate new revenue for your store, but merely steals revenue from a product you already stock.
9.1 The Substitution Edge (The Zero-Sum Mathematics)
In the OASIS neural network, a Cannibal is identified by a high density of substitution edges. Unlike a link edge (which builds a basket), a substitution edge is a zero-sum war between two nodes for the exact same slot in the basket.
To measure the toxicity of a new product a supplier is pitching, the algorithmic operator calculates the Cannibalization Rate (CR):
$$CR = \frac{\text{Volume Stolen from Existing SKUs}}{\text{Total Volume of New SKU}}$$
The Scenario: You currently sell 100 bottles of PEPTANG TOMATO SAUCE a week. A supplier convinces you to introduce ZESTY TOMATO SAUCE.
The Result: After one month, you check the POS data. You sold 20 bottles of Zesty. The beginner celebrates the 20 new sales. But the expert looks at the Peptang data and sees it dropped from 100 bottles to 80 bottles.
The Math: The volume stolen from Peptang is 20. The total volume of Zesty is 20.
$$CR = \frac{20}{20} = 1.0 \text{ (or 100\%)}$$
The Verdict: The Cannibalization Rate is 100%. The new product generated absolutely zero incremental lift for the store. It simply cannibalized the existing sales. The expert immediately delists Zesty, returns the stock, and frees up the working capital.
9.2 The Line Extension Trap (The Flavor Illusion)
Tier 1 suppliers (The Apex Dictators) rarely try to sell you entirely new brands; instead, they attack your shelf space using Line Extensions.
The Setup: A dominant snack supplier sells a highly profitable "Salted" crisp and a "Tomato" crisp. These are high-velocity Predators. Suddenly, the supplier launches "Spicy BBQ," "Cheese & Onion," and "Sweet Chili" flavors, demanding you list all of them.
The Beginner Execution: The beginner assumes that because the Salted flavor sells fast, the BBQ flavor will also sell fast. They clear shelf space and list all five flavors.
The Financial Reality: The customer who usually buys two packets of Salted crisps on a Friday now buys one Salted and one BBQ. The total unit volume for the supplier remains exactly the same. However, the supplier has now secured five facings on your shelf instead of two, locking out their competitors. You are now holding five boxes of inventory in your backroom instead of two. Your holding costs have skyrocketed, your GMROI has plummeted, and the supplier has successfully transferred their warehouse costs onto your balance sheet.
9.3 Asymmetric Cannibalization (The Triage Protocol)
As we established in the Deep Web analysis, substitution is rarely equal. It is directional. Understanding the direction of a substitution edge is how an expert operator survives a cash-flow crisis without losing customers.
Symmetric Substitution: Two identical products (e.g., two brands of generic 500g table salt). If Brand A is out of stock, 100% of customers buy Brand B. If Brand B is out of stock, 100% buy Brand A. Execution: You never stock both. Pick the one with the best credit terms and delist the other.
Asymmetric Substitution (Trade-Down Gravity): Customers are highly willing to "trade down" to a cheaper alternative if forced, but fiercely resist "trading up" to a more expensive one.
If you stock out of a premium 1,500 KES imported pasta, the customer will sigh and buy the 200 KES local pasta.
If you stock out of the 200 KES local pasta, the budget-constrained customer will not buy the 1,500 KES imported pasta. They will leave the store and go to the duka next door.
The Triage Execution: If your store is bleeding cash and you must halt procurement to survive the week, you intentionally stock out of the Premium (Trade-Down) nodes. You use Asymmetric Cannibalization as a weapon. You force the premium buyer into the cheaper node, consolidating your volume into the cheapest, most capital-efficient SKU, keeping the foot traffic alive while your bank account recovers.
9.4 The Physical Firewall (Category Capping)
You cannot fight Cannibalization using willpower; suppliers are too aggressive, and human store managers are too easily swayed by promotional discounts. You must fight Cannibalization using hard physical architecture.
This is the Category Cap.
Calculate the Assortment Limit: The OASIS engine analyzes a physical shelf (e.g., the [[WINES]] gondola). It determines that mathematically, you only need 18 distinct SKUs to capture 95% of all consumer taste profiles (Sweet, Dry, Red, White, varying price points).
Build the Firewall: You physically construct the shelving to hold exactly 18 facings. You do not leave a single inch of dead space.
The One-In, One-Out Mandate: When a distributor representative arrives with a new wine, you point to the shelf. It is physically full. You tell the rep: "I have an Assortment Limit of 18. If you want this new wine on the shelf, you must show me the POS data proving which of your existing wines I should permanently delist to make room for it."
By enforcing a physical firewall, you force the supplier to cannibalize their own portfolio, rather than cannibalizing your working capital.
