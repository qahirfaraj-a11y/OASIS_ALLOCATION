# Chapter 1: The Retail Continuum (The Physics of Physical Space)

To the absolute beginner, retail looks simple: buy goods at a low price, put them on a shelf, and sell them at a higher price. But a retail store is not a storage unit for boxes. It is a psychological funnel designed to manipulate human movement and capture cash flow.
In Kenya, retail formats do not evolve in a straight line. The massive Mega-Store does not replace the neighborhood corner shop. Instead, they exist at the exact same time, targeting the exact same consumer, just at different points in their monthly salary cycle. If you do not understand the specific "physics" of your store's size, you will bankrupt yourself trying to run a corner shop like a supermarket.
1.1 The Micro-Duka: The High-Velocity Survival Engine
A standard duka (kiosk) operates in a space often smaller than 100 square feet. To a beginner, it looks like a chaotic, informal setup. To an expert, it is a hyper-efficient cash engine built for the "Kadogo" (small) economy.
The Psychology: The primary duka customer is a daily wage earner, or a middle-class consumer needing an immediate emergency item at 8:00 PM. They cannot afford to lock up their money in a 30-day supply of groceries. They buy for the next 12 hours.
The Format Constraints: A duka uses a "Service Window" layout. The customer stands outside; the operator fetches the goods. This means the customer cannot browse.
The SKU Strategy (The Expert View): Because space is tiny, a duka can only stock about 150 items. You cannot stock slow-moving items here. A duka survives on Traffic Engines (like Brookside 500ml milk). In larger supermarkets, this milk is a "Loss Leader" sold at a negative margin (-7.88%) just to get people in the door. But in a duka, the operator applies a Convenience Premium—marking the price up slightly because the customer won't walk 3 kilometers in the dark to save 5 shillings.
The Trap: The operator uses that milk to bring the customer to the window. While the customer waits for change, they stare at the wire mesh. This is where the operator hangs the Impulse Closer (like a Mr. Berrys Lollipop at a 33% profit margin). The milk pays the distributor; the lollipop buys the operator's dinner.
1.2 The Estate Minimart: The Browsing Trap
The Minimart (500 to 2,000 square feet) sits inside residential estates. It is built for the "Mid-Week Top-Up." The customer comes in on a Wednesday evening for fresh bread, milk, and maybe a cleaning spray.
The Psychology: This is the first format where the customer holds a basket and walks inside the store. They transition from "immediate need" to "browsing."
The Format Constraints: This format gives the operator the "Illusion of Space." A beginner operator sees an empty shelf and thinks they need to fill it with variety.
The SKU Strategy (The Expert View): This is the most dangerous format in retail. It is the prime breeding ground for Cannibalization. Because the operator has space, a supplier will convince them to stock 15 different brands of mid-tier wine. But the customer walking in on a Wednesday just wants any decent red wine. By stocking 15 varieties, the operator doesn't sell more wine; they just spread the same number of sales across 15 bottles. The total revenue stays the same, but the operator has trapped 15 times more of their own cash in inventory that sits on the shelf gathering dust. Minimarts require ruthless "Assortment Capping"—if you only need 4 brands of wine to satisfy the customer, you strictly ban the other 11 from your store.
1.3 The Mega-Store: The Shadow Bank
The Mega-Store (10,000+ square feet) is a logistical beast. It does not survive on daily convenience; it survives on the Payday Pulse—the end-of-month salary cycle when consumers buy their monthly bulk items.
The Psychology: Destination shopping. The customer is willing to drive past ten dukas and three minimarts because the Mega-Store guarantees they can get everything on their list in one trip.
The Format Constraints: The biggest threat to a Mega-Store is the "Dead Zone." If a store is 20,000 square feet, a customer will naturally try to walk the shortest path, ignoring 80% of the aisles.
The SKU Strategy (The Expert View): To fix Dead Zones, experts use Anchor Dispersal. You take the heavy, negative-margin staples that everyone must buy—like 2kg Soko Flour and 5L Rina Cooking Oil—and you place them in the absolute furthest, opposite corners of the massive building. You force the customer to walk diagonally across the entire store, dragging them past the high-margin "Profit Stabilizers" (like premium spices and imported cosmetics).
